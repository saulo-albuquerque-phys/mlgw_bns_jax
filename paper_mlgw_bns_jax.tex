\begin{abstract}
We present \texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax}, a complete reimplementation of the
\texttt{mlgw\_bns} gravitational waveform surrogate in the JAX framework.
Our model preserves the full accuracy of the original --- achieving
machine-precision agreement (mismatch $\bar{\mathcal{F}} \sim 10^{-14}$)
--- while gaining JIT compilation, automatic differentiation, hardware-agnostic
GPU/TPU acceleration, and native compatibility with vectorized samplers
through \texttt{jax.vmap}.  We demonstrate its integration with the
\textsc{SHARPy} sampler for sequential Monte~Carlo parameter estimation of
the binary neutron star transient GW170817.  Furthermore, we construct a
reduced-order quadrature (ROQ) basis --- both via \texttt{JenpyROQ} and
with our own JAX-native ROQ builder that exploits \texttt{jax.jit} and
\texttt{jax.vmap} for efficient, parallelised basis construction --- and
implement a JAX-native ROQ likelihood that evaluates waveform templates only
at $\mathcal{O}(10^2)$ empirical interpolation nodes rather than the full
$\sim 2.5 \times 10^5$ frequency grid, yielding $\sim\!100\times$ memory
reduction per sampler particle.  The combination of JAX acceleration, ROQ
compression, and GPU-parallel sampling opens the path toward real-time
parameter estimation for binary neutron star mergers in the era of
next-generation detectors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Bayesian parameter estimation (PE) of gravitational wave (GW) signals from
binary neutron star (BNS) mergers requires the generation of $\gtrsim 10^7$
waveform templates~\cite{Veitch:2014wba,Breschi:2021wzr}.  The computational
cost of this procedure is dominated by two factors: the per-template
generation time $t_{\rm wf}$ and the evaluation of likelihood inner products
$t_{\rm ip}$, both of which scale linearly with the number of frequency grid
points $N_{\rm freq}$.

Tissino et al.~\cite{Tissino:2022} introduced \texttt{mlgw\_bns}, a
machine-learning surrogate trained on the state-of-the-art effective-one-body
(EOB) model \texttt{TEOBResumSPA}, achieving waveform generation speedups of
$\sim\!35\times$ at reduced frequency grids.  When combined with
reduced-order quadratures (ROQs) constructed with \texttt{JenpyROQ}%
~\cite{JenpyROQ}, an overall PE acceleration of more than an order of
magnitude was demonstrated for the analysis of GW170817.

However, the original \texttt{mlgw\_bns} is implemented in NumPy/SciPy with
\texttt{numba} just-in-time compilation, which limits it to CPU execution and
prevents seamless integration with modern gradient-based and vectorized
sampling algorithms.  In particular, the growing class of sequential Monte
Carlo (SMC) samplers --- such as \textsc{SHARPy}~\cite{SHARPy} --- exploit
\texttt{jax.vmap} to evaluate the likelihood across hundreds of particles
simultaneously on GPU, achieving dramatic wall-clock speedups.  To unlock
these capabilities, the waveform model itself must be natively expressed as a
JAX computation graph.

In this work, we present \texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax}, a standalone JAX
reimplementation of \texttt{mlgw\_bns} that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item preserves machine-precision agreement with the original model
          ($\bar{\mathcal{F}} \sim 10^{-14}$);
    \item is fully JIT-compilable, automatically differentiable, and
          \texttt{vmap}-compatible;
    \item requires no dependency on the original \texttt{mlgw\_bns} package
          at prediction time;
    \item integrates natively with \textsc{SHARPy} for GPU-accelerated PE;
    \item supports an ROQ-compressed likelihood where templates are evaluated
          only at empirical interpolation nodes.
\end{enumerate}

This paper is organized as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:original} briefly
reviews the original \texttt{mlgw\_bns} model.  Section~\ref{sec:jax}
describes the JAX reimplementation.  Section~\ref{sec:accuracy} presents
accuracy validation.  Section~\ref{sec:speed} discusses computational
performance.  Section~\ref{sec:roq} covers the ROQ basis construction and
JAX-native likelihood.  Section~\ref{sec:pe} presents parameter estimation
results for GW170817 with \textsc{SHARPy}.  Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}
concludes.

\section{The original \texttt{mlgw\_bns} model}
\label{sec:original}

We briefly summarize the key aspects of \texttt{mlgw\_bns} as introduced
in~\cite{Tissino:2022}; the reader is referred there for full details.

\texttt{mlgw\_bns} is a frequency-domain surrogate for the $(2,2)$ mode of
BNS waveforms, trained on \texttt{TEOBResumSPA}.  The model learns the
residuals of waveform amplitude and phase relative to a TaylorF2
post-Newtonian (PN) baseline:
\begin{align}
    \Delta A(f;\bm{\theta}) &= \log\!\left(\frac{A_{\rm EOB}}{A_{\rm PN}}\right), \label{eq:resA}\\
    \Delta\phi(f;\bm{\theta}) &= \phi_{\rm EOB}(f;\bm{\theta}) - \phi_{\rm PN}(f;\bm{\theta}), \label{eq:resphi}
\end{align}
where the intrinsic parameters are $\bm{\theta} = \{q, \chi_1, \chi_2,
\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2\}$.  The total mass $M$ is factored out via the
dimensionless frequency $Mf$, with a reference mass $M_{\rm ref} = 2.8\,
M_\odot$.

The dimensionality of the residual representation is reduced in three stages:
(i)~greedy downsampling of the frequency grid from $\sim\!10^7$ to
$\sim\!10^3$ points; (ii)~principal component analysis (PCA) compression to
30~components; (iii)~a shallow neural network (MLP) that maps
$\bm{\theta} \to \tilde{\bm{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^{30}$.

The PN baseline includes 3.5PN-accurate amplitude, pseudo-5.5PN phase, tidal
contributions to 7.5PN, and the monopole-quadrupole 3PN phase correction.
The model achieves mismatches $\bar{\mathcal{F}} \lesssim 10^{-5}$ against
\texttt{TEOBResumSPA} when trained with $1.3\times10^5$ waveforms.

\section{JAX reimplementation}
\label{sec:jax}

\subsection{Motivation}

The original \texttt{mlgw\_bns} relies on \texttt{scikit-learn} for the
neural network, \texttt{scipy.interpolate} for cubic splines, and
\texttt{numba} for selected hot loops.  While performant on CPU, this
stack is fundamentally incompatible with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GPU/TPU execution}: none of the above libraries target
          accelerator hardware;
    \item \textbf{Automatic differentiation}: gradients $\partial h /
          \partial\bm{\theta}$ are unavailable, precluding Hamiltonian Monte
          Carlo and Fisher matrix analyses;
    \item \textbf{Vectorized sampling}: \texttt{jax.vmap} requires all
          operations within the likelihood to be expressed as JAX primitives.
\end{itemize}

JAX~\cite{jax2018github} provides all three capabilities within a NumPy-like
API, making it the natural choice for a reimplementation.

\subsection{Architecture}

\texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax} is a single-file, standalone module
(\texttt{jax\_import\_n\_predict.py}) with no dependency on
\texttt{mlgw\_bns}.  A separate export script (\texttt{jax\_export.py})
serializes all trained model data --- neural network weights, PCA
eigenvectors, downsampled frequency grids --- into an HDF5 file.

The prediction pipeline, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, proceeds as
follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input normalization}: the 5 intrinsic parameters
          $\bm{\theta}$ are standardized using stored mean and scale vectors.

    \item \textbf{Neural network evaluation}: two hidden layers
          ($5{\to}82{\to}95{\to}30$) with $\tanh$ activation and linear
          output yield 30 PCA-scaled components.  The total parameter count
          is 11,847.

    \item \textbf{PCA reconstruction}: the 30 components are de-scaled by
          eigenvalues raised to the power $\alpha = 0.368$, then projected
          back to the 6,197-dimensional residual space via the stored
          eigenvectors.

    \item \textbf{Residual separation}: the first 5,046 values correspond to
          amplitude residuals at the downsampled frequency grid; the
          remaining 1,151 to phase residuals.

    \item \textbf{PN baseline evaluation}: the TaylorF2 amplitude
          (\texttt{\_Af3hPN\_jax}, 3.5PN) and phase
          (\texttt{\_Phif5hPN\_jax}, pseudo-5.5PN + tidal 7.5PN +
          monopole-quadrupole 3PN) are evaluated at the downsampled
          frequencies, with mass rescaling applied.

    \item \textbf{Residual recombination}:
          \begin{align}
              A(f) &= A_{\rm PN}(f)\,\exp[\Delta A_{\rm pred}(f)],\\
              \phi(f) &= \phi_{\rm PN}(f) + \Delta\phi_{\rm pred}(f).
          \end{align}

    \item \textbf{Cubic spline interpolation}: amplitude and phase are
          interpolated from the downsampled grid to user-specified
          frequencies using a JAX-native natural cubic spline.  The
          tridiagonal system is solved with \texttt{jax.lax.scan}, ensuring
          the spline evaluation is JIT-compilable and differentiable.

    \item \textbf{Polarization computation}: the plus and cross polarizations
          are assembled with the appropriate inclination-angle factors.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Key implementation details}

\paragraph{Cubic spline via \texttt{lax.scan}.}
The natural cubic spline requires solving a tridiagonal linear system.
Standard SciPy routines are not JIT-compilable.  We implement the Thomas
algorithm using two \texttt{jax.lax.scan} passes (forward elimination and
back-substitution), yielding a fully differentiable spline that can be
composed with \texttt{vmap} over batches of parameters.

\paragraph{Float64 precision.}
Gravitational-wave phase accuracy demands double precision.  We enforce
\texttt{jax.config.update("jax\_enable\_x64", True)} at module load.

\paragraph{Zero external dependencies.}
At prediction time, only \texttt{jax}, \texttt{numpy} (for HDF5 loading), and
\texttt{h5py} are required.  The \texttt{mlgw\_bns} package is needed only
once, during the export step.

\section{Computational performance}
\label{sec:speed}

The computational advantages of the JAX reimplementation arise from three
mechanisms: JIT compilation, GPU offloading, and vectorization.

\subsection{JIT compilation}

On first call, \texttt{jax.jit} traces the full prediction pipeline and
compiles it to optimized XLA~\cite{XLA} kernels.  Subsequent calls avoid
Python interpreter overhead entirely.  For a single waveform on $N = 1000$
frequency points, the JIT-compiled evaluation on CPU takes
\tbd{X}\,ms, compared to \tbd{Y}\,ms for the original
\texttt{mlgw\_bns}.

\subsection{GPU acceleration}

The compiled computation graph executes without modification on NVIDIA GPUs.
For a batch of $B$ waveforms evaluated simultaneously via \texttt{vmap}:
\begin{equation}
    t_{\rm batch}(B) \approx t_{\rm overhead} + B \cdot t_{\rm per\text{-}wf}^{\rm GPU},
\end{equation}
where $t_{\rm per\text{-}wf}^{\rm GPU}$ is significantly smaller than the CPU
counterpart due to SIMD parallelism.  Table~\ref{tab:speed} reports
representative timings.

\subsection{Automatic differentiation}

Since every operation in the pipeline is a JAX primitive, gradients
$\partial h_{+,\times}/\partial\bm{\theta}$ are obtained for free via
\texttt{jax.grad}.  This enables:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fisher matrix forecasts without finite-difference approximations;
    \item Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling;
    \item gradient-based template bank placement~\cite{Coogan:2022}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reduced-order quadrature for JAX-native PE}
\label{sec:roq}

\subsection{ROQ basis construction}

We construct ROQ interpolants for \texttt{mlgw\_bns} using
\texttt{JenpyROQ}~\cite{JenpyROQ}, following the methodology
of~\cite{Tissino:2022} but with an updated configuration targeting the
GW170817 parameter space.  The key settings are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Frequency range: $[23, 2000]$\,Hz, segment length $T = 128$\,s,
          yielding $N_{\rm freq} = 253{,}057$ grid points;
    \item Chirp mass range: $\mathcal{M}_c \in [1.18, 1.21]\,M_\odot$;
    \item Tolerance: $10^{-4}$ (linear), $10^{-6}$ (quadratic);
    \item Three enrichment cycles with $10^4$, $5\times10^4$, $10^5$
          training waveforms.
\end{itemize}

For the basis construction we use the NumPy wrapper
(\texttt{mlgw\_bns\_roq\_wrapper.py}) to avoid JAX's LLVM memory overhead
during the generation of $\sim\!10^5$ training waveforms.

\subsection{JAX-native ROQ builder}
\label{sec:roq_jax}

While \texttt{JenpyROQ} provides a well-tested reference implementation, its
serial Python loop over training waveforms can become a bottleneck when the
parameter space or the number of enrichment cycles grows.  We therefore
developed an independent, JAX-native ROQ basis builder
(\texttt{roq\_builder\_jax.py}) that implements the same
algorithmic pipeline of~\cite{Field:2014ROQ}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-selection (greedy basis).}  A pool of corner and
          random parameter-space samples is scanned in a streaming fashion.
          At each greedy iteration the waveform with the largest projection
          error $\|h - \mathcal{P}_V h\|^2$ onto the current orthonormal
          basis~$V$ is identified and appended via modified Gram--Schmidt
          with re-orthogonalization.
    \item \textbf{Enrichment.}  Successively larger training sets
          ($10^4$, $5\times10^4$, $10^5$ waveforms) are generated
          on-the-fly and streamed through the projection-error test.
          Any waveform whose error exceeds the tolerance is added to the
          basis immediately, so the basis grows monotonically without
          requiring all training waveforms in memory.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Interpolation Method (EIM).}  Given the
          reduced basis $\{e_j\}_{j=1}^m$, the standard EIM
          algorithm~\cite{Field:2014ROQ} selects a sparse set of frequency
          nodes and constructs the interpolant matrix~$B$ such that
          $h(f) \approx B\, h(\{f_j^{\rm EIM}\})$ for any representable
          waveform.
\end{enumerate}

The key optimizations that distinguish the JAX implementation from the
original \texttt{JenpyROQ} pipeline are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{JIT-compiled waveform kernels.}
          Both a single-waveform function and a \texttt{jax.vmap}-batched
          version are compiled to XLA machine code \emph{once} before any
          training-set evaluation (warm-up phase).  All subsequent calls
          reuse the cached compiled kernels, eliminating Python-level
          overhead entirely.

    \item \textbf{Vectorised evaluation via \texttt{jax.vmap}.}
          Waveforms are generated in small batches (default batch size~8
          for $253$\,k-point grids) through a \texttt{vmap}'d kernel.
          The batch size is kept deliberately small to bound peak memory at
          $\sim\!2$\,GB of intermediates, making the build feasible on
          machines with $\sim\!16$\,GB RAM.

    \item \textbf{Streaming memory management.}
          Training sets are never fully materialised.  Both the greedy
          pre-selection and enrichment loops generate and discard waveforms
          in projection batches (default~200), keeping only the current
          orthonormal basis ($\sim\!100 \times 253\text{k} \times 16$\,B
          $\approx 400$\,MB) and one batch in memory at a time.
          Explicit garbage collection is triggered after every batch to
          prevent heap fragmentation.

    \item \textbf{Checkpoint / resume support.}
          A JSON status marker is written after each completed phase
          (pre-selection, enrichment, EIM) for each basis kind
          (linear, quadratic).  If the process is interrupted --- e.g.\
          due to time limits on shared clusters --- it can be restarted
          and will automatically skip phases whose checkpoints are
          present on disk.  This is especially important for the
          enrichment stage, which may require several hours for
          $\sim\!10^5$ waveforms on CPU.
\end{itemize}

The procedure is executed independently for the linear basis (acting on
$h(f)$) and the quadratic basis (acting on~$|h(f)|^2$).  The output files
are identical in format to those produced by \texttt{JenpyROQ} and can be
used interchangeably for ROQ-accelerated parameter estimation.

\subsection{Node-only waveform evaluation}

The standard ROQ likelihood decomposes the Wiener inner products as:
\begin{align}
    \langle d | h \rangle &\approx \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm lin}}
        w_j^{(\rm lin)}\, h(f_j^{\rm lin};\bm{\theta}), \label{eq:dh}\\
    \langle h | h \rangle &\approx \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\rm qua}}
        w_j^{(\rm qua)}\, |h(f_j^{\rm qua};\bm{\theta})|^2, \label{eq:hh}
\end{align}
where $\{f_j^{\rm lin}\}$ and $\{f_j^{\rm qua}\}$ are the empirical
interpolation nodes, and the weights $w_j$ are pre-computed from the basis
interpolants and the detector data/PSD.

A crucial optimization is that the waveform template $h$ needs only be
evaluated at the empirical nodes --- \emph{not} on the full $N_{\rm freq}$
grid.  With $n_{\rm lin} \sim 250$ and $n_{\rm qua} \sim 10$, this
represents a $\sim\!1000\times$ reduction in the number of frequency points
per likelihood evaluation, and a $\sim\!100\times$ reduction in memory per
sampler particle.

In our implementation, the \texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax} \texttt{predict()} function
is called twice per likelihood evaluation:
\begin{lstlisting}
h_lin = predict(params, f_lin_jax, M, D, iota)
h_qua = predict(params, f_qua_jax, M, D, iota)
\end{lstlisting}
Both calls operate on $\mathcal{O}(10^2)$ frequency points rather than
$\mathcal{O}(10^5)$, and are JIT-compiled and \texttt{vmap}'d across sampler
particles.

\subsection{ROQ weights}

The weights are pre-computed once before sampling:
\begin{align}
    w_j^{(\rm lin)} &= \sum_k \bar{B}^{(\rm lin)}_{jk}\,
        \frac{4\,\Delta f}{S_n(f_k)}, \\
    d_j^{(\rm roq)} &= \sum_k \bar{B}^{(\rm lin)}_{jk}\,
        \frac{\bar{d}_k \cdot 4\,\Delta f}{S_n(f_k)}, \\
    w_j^{(\rm qua)} &= \sum_k \bar{B}^{(\rm qua)}_{jk}\,
        \frac{4\,\Delta f}{S_n(f_k)},
\end{align}
where $B^{(\rm lin/qua)}$ are the basis interpolant matrices output by
\texttt{JenpyROQ}, $d_k$ is the detector data, and $S_n(f_k)$ the PSD.
The constant term $\langle d | d \rangle$ is also pre-computed.  All weights
are stored as JAX arrays on GPU.

\subsection{Files required for PE}

The ROQ build produces large basis files ($\gtrsim 400$\,MB) that are only
needed during weight pre-computation.  For the actual sampling, only the
small files are required:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Empirical frequencies: \texttt{empirical\_frequencies\_\{linear,quadratic\}.npy}
    \item Empirical nodes: \texttt{empirical\_nodes\_\{linear,quadratic\}.npy}
    \item Basis interpolants: \texttt{basis\_interpolant\_\{linear,quadratic\}.npy}
\end{itemize}
These total $\mathcal{O}(10)$\,MB and can be packaged for use in
cloud-based GPU environments such as Google Colab.

\section{Parameter estimation of GW170817 with \textsc{SHARPy}}
\label{sec:pe}

\subsection{The \textsc{SHARPy} sampler}

\textsc{SHARPy}~\cite{SHARPy} is a sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) sampler
designed for gravitational-wave inference.  It evolves a population of
weighted particles through a sequence of tempered distributions, using
Hamiltonian-like kernels for the rejuvenation step.  Its key feature is that
the likelihood evaluation for all particles is vectorized via
\texttt{jax.vmap}, enabling massive parallelism on GPU.

This vectorization requires the entire likelihood function --- including
waveform generation, antenna pattern projection, and time-delay computation
--- to be expressed as JAX primitives.  \textsc{SHARPy}'s utility functions
(antenna patterns, time delays, sidereal time) are already implemented in
\texttt{jax.numpy}, and \texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax} completes the JAX-native
stack.

\subsection{Analysis setup}

We analyse the deglitched GWOSC data~\cite{GWOSC} for GW170817, centered at
GPS time $1187008857$, with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Detectors: LIGO Hanford (H1), LIGO Livingston (L1), Virgo (V1);
    \item Frequency range: $[23, 2000]$\,Hz;
    \item Segment length: $T = 128$\,s;
    \item Waveform: \texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax};
    \item Sampler: \textsc{SHARPy} with \tbd{N} particles;
    \item Sky position: fixed to EM counterpart
          ($\alpha = 3.446$\,rad, $\delta = -0.408$\,rad).
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have presented \texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax}, a JAX-native reimplementation of
the \texttt{mlgw\_bns} BNS waveform surrogate.  The key results are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy}: machine-precision agreement with the original
          model (mismatch $\sim\!10^{-14}$), preserving the
          $\bar{\mathcal{F}} \lesssim 10^{-5}$ faithfulness to
          \texttt{TEOBResumSPA}.

    \item \textbf{Differentiability}: end-to-end automatic differentiation
          enables gradient-based sampling and Fisher matrix computations.

    \item \textbf{GPU acceleration}: native execution on GPU/TPU hardware,
          with \texttt{vmap} vectorization across sampler particles.

    \item \textbf{ROQ compression}: waveform evaluation at
          $\mathcal{O}(10^2)$ empirical nodes instead of
          $\mathcal{O}(10^5)$ grid points, with $\sim\!100\times$ memory
          reduction.  In addition to the \texttt{JenpyROQ}-based
          construction, we provide a JAX-native ROQ builder that exploits
          \texttt{jax.jit} and \texttt{jax.vmap} for parallelized basis
          construction with streaming memory management and checkpoint /
          resume support (Sec.~\ref{sec:roq_jax}).

    \item \textbf{SHARPy integration}: full compatibility with the
          \textsc{SHARPy} SMC sampler for GPU-parallel PE.
\end{enumerate}

The combination of these advances positions \texttt{mlgw\_bns\_jax} as a
practical tool for routine BNS PE with accurate EOB-level waveforms,
both for current LIGO--Virgo--KAGRA analyses and for the next generation of
ground-based detectors.

Future directions include: (i)~extending the model to higher harmonics and
precessing spins; (ii)~coupling with post-merger models; (iii)~exploiting
automatic differentiation for Hamiltonian nested sampling;
(iv)~GPU-accelerated ROQ weight computation for multi-detector networks.
